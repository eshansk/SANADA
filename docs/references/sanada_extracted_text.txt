1. Introduction
Speech separation, sound counting, and acoustic tagging are increasingly
required to operate together in dense scenes: meeting rooms, lecture halls,
and city streets where speakers, transient events, and ambient processes over-
lap without pause. Solving all three tasks jointly demands that a system
simultaneouslyseparateconstituent sources,countactive entities, andrecog-
nisetheir semantic classes from a single channel. Deep learning has ad-
vanced each task individually over the past decade [1]. Permutation invari-
ant training (PIT) [2, 3] and deep clustering [4] established the foundations
for talker-independent separation. Conv-TasNet [5] and dual-path RNNs [6]
then moved processing entirely into the time domain, yielding large gains on
two- and three-speaker benchmarks. More recently, HDemucs [7], the Audio
Spectrogram Transformer [8], and Conformer networks [9] extended these
gains to classification and regression settings. Each of these architectures,
however, has been validated almost exclusively on corpora with a fixed, low
speaker count, leaving their behaviour under high source density untested.

1.1. Structural Gaps in Existing Corpora
Studying separation, counting, and tagging in realistic overlapping scenes
requires datasets that span a range of source densities. Core speech separa-
tion benchmarks such as WSJ0-2mix/3mix [10], LibriMix [11], WHAM! [12],
and WHAMR! [13] provide high-quality mixture–stem pairs and have driven
much of the progress in multi-speaker models. Their mixtures, however, are
limited to a small, fixed number of speakers, typicallyK≤3, so they do
not reveal how architectures behave as the number of simultaneous sources
increases. Universal and noise-robust corpora such as FUSS [14] extend be-
yond read speech and support domain-agnostic separation [15, 16], but they
still lack a controlled, variable-Kprogression comparable to conversational
settings. Large-scale audio datasets including FSD50K [17], AudioSet [18],
and ESC [19] emphasise label diversity and have been effective for sound
event tagging, yet they consist mainly of mixed recordings without isolated
stems or precise control over source overlap. Without pre-mix reference sig-
nals, standard separation metrics such as scale-invariant signal-to-distortion
ratio (SI-SDR) [20] cannot be computed reliably in high-density conditions.
1.2. The SANADA Suite & Key Contributions
Toaddressthisdatasetgap, weintroduceSANADA(Samslesha-Nada)[21],
a reproducible set of synthetic acoustic corpora designed for variable-Kmix-
tures. Org-Mix-4 and Org-Mix-9 cover organisational speech with up to nine
simultaneous speakers, while FSD-Mix extends to 32 environmental sound
classes. Together they expose regimes in which fixed-output models struggle
when the number of active sources is unknown or large. SANADA is built
around four design goals:

2. Related Work
Conv-TasNet [5], SepFormer [24], and MossFormer2 [25] represent a
clear architectural progression: from time-frequency masking toward time-
domain convolution and transformer-based attention. Yet the benchmarks
used to evaluate these models have not kept pace. Most corpora fixKat two
or three sources, so the behaviour of these architectures under higher source
counts remains largely unknown.
2.1. Deep Learning for Speech and Audio Source Separation
Speech separation corpora such as LibriMix [11] and WHAMR! [13]
mainly provide mixtures with two or three speakers. They are therefore
not representative of organisational or environmental scenes in which the
number of active sourcesKis unknown and can be much larger. At the
other extreme, large-scale tagging datasets such as AudioSet [18] offer broad
class coverage but do not include isolated stems, making them unsuitable
for evaluating separation metrics. Many existing benchmarks also emphasise

2.2. Speech Mixture Datasets
Most work on monaural speech separation has been driven by a small set
of low-density corpora. Two-speaker (WSJ0-2mix) and three-speaker (WSJ0-
3mix) mixtures, derived from WSJ0 [26], are the standard testbeds for PIT,
deep clustering, and Conv-TasNet-style models [2, 4, 5]. LibriMix [11] ex-
tends this setup with additional noise conditions and sampling rates, while
WHAM! [12] and WHAMR! [13] add background noise and room reverber-
ation. In all of these corpora, however, the number of simultaneous speakers
is fixed atK= 2orK= 3, so they do not support systematic evaluation
of separation models as the speaker count increases to higher values such as
K= 8orK= 9.
2.3. General Audio Tagging and Sound Event Datasets
Large-scale audio datasets are typically designed for classification rather
than controlled mixture modelling. FSD50K [17] and FSDKaggle2018 [27],
both derived from Freesound [23], provide single-channel clips with dense
labels across many classes using the AudioSet ontology [18]. These resources
have been central to recent progress in sound event tagging, but they do
not supply isolated stems or explicit control over the number of overlapping
sources. Environmental benchmarks such as ESC-50 [19] and domestic scene
datasets such as DESED [28] share the same limitation. As a result, they

2.4. Universal and Music Separation Datasets
Universal separation datasets attempt to cross acoustic domains within
a unified generation framework. FUSS [14] synthesizes mixtures of arbitrary
sound events with varying degrees of overlap, serving as the primary bench-
mark for domain-agnostic separation networks [15, 16]. However, FUSS is
heavilygearedtowardgenericsoundextractionandlacksthecontrolled, high-
density variable-Kprogression required to simulate crowded conversational
or organizational speech scenarios. In the music domain, MUSDB18 [29]
provides multitrack mixtures and stems, but is strictly constrained to a
small, fixed taxonomy of instruments (vocals, drums, bass, accompaniment).
Slakh [30] extends this concept using synthetic MIDI-derived audio, en-
abling robust training data ablations. While invaluable for algorithmic music
separation, these corpora do not encompass speech or broad environmen-
tal domains, nor do they scaleKdynamically to simulate chaotic, uncon-
strained acoustic interference. Crucially, while existing standard corpora like
MUSDB18 [29] and FUSS [14] arbitrarily plateau at maximum densities of
K≤4, SANADA dynamically scales toK= 32, explicitly isolating the
extreme-density variable-Kgap. A comprehensive comparison of these ar-
chitectural limits is detailed in Table 1, the proposed Org-Mix-4/Org-Mix-9
and FSD-Mix suite resolves these critical deficits. By providing variable-K
mixtures scaling up toK= 32, paired with absolute ground-truth stems and
unified metadata across both speech and sound event domains, SANADA
establishes a rigorous continuum for high-density machine listening.

4. Generative Pipeline & Acoustic Mixing Methodology
The SANADA pipeline generates mixtures for both fixed-Kand variable-
Ksettings while keeping conditions comparable across speech and environ-
mental domains. Because the source material comes from web lecture audio
and crowdsourced environmental recordings, the design emphasises avoiding
data leakage and unintended cross-domain mixing. As outlined in Fig. 1,
the process is organised into three stages: domain-specific source acquisi-
tion (Phase 1), pre-mix partitioning and temporal windowing (Phase 2), and
single-channel mixing with controlled overlap and attenuation (Phase 3).

4.2. Phase 2: Temporal Segmentation & Leakage Prevention
After source acquisition, SANADA applies dataset splitting and temporal
windowing before any mixing takes place. Many synthetic benchmarks cre-
ate mixtures first and then divide them into train, validation, and test sets;
this post-mix splitting allows the same speaker or event instance to influ-
ence multiple splits and can inflate reported metrics [35, 11]. In SANADA,
partitioning is performed at the raw-file level before trimming or mixing.
4.2.1. Wing 1: Pre-Mix Data Partitioning
For organisational speech, the long-form audio inMultichannel/and
Multichannel9/is divided using a fixed random seed. To support repro-
ducible experiments across frameworks [31], the pseudo-random number gen-
erator is initialised withS= 42. For each speaker directory (for exam-
ple,Speaker_4_1orSpeaker1), the script shuffles the list of files and ap-
plies a 70/15/15 split for train/validation/test. Files are then copied into
Multichannel4Speakers/andMultichannel9Speakers/, each with sepa-
ratetrain/,val/, andtest/subdirectories. Any mixture generated for
the test set therefore uses utterances that never appear in the training or
validation sets. Algorithm 3 summarises this procedure.
4.2.2. Wing 1: Temporal Windowing and Artifact Exclusion
Once the file-level split is fixed, the lecture recordings (typically around
15 minutes long) are converted into fixed-length segments for model train-
ing [31]. Boundary regions of web audio often contain music, applause, or

4.3.1. Algorithmic Superposition and Overlap
In dense environments such as meetings or streets, sources often overlap
for long periods and mask each other spectrally [13]. For all mixtures with
K≥2, SANADA keeps every source active across the full 8.0-second window.

4.3.3. Unified Metadata During Mixing
During mixing, the engine also writes ametadata.csvfile (Algorithm 5).

value ofK, the source stem paths, and summary level statistics (average

4.3.4. Mixture Quotas and Combinatorial Scale
The stem pools created in Phase 2 are large (for example, 1,000/500/500
stems per speaker), and enumerating all combinations would produce an im-
practically large corpus. Instead, the mixer draws a fixed number of mixtures
per configuration. For each dataset variant (e.g.,Org-Mix-4,Org-Mix-9,
FSD-Mix-Variable), the engine generates:
•1,000 training mixtures (from the 70% training stems),
•100 validation mixtures (from the 15% validation stems),
•100 test mixtures (from the 15% test stems).
This scheme keeps train/validation/test completely disjoint, while providing
consistent dataset sizes across all tasks.

6. Discussion and Limitations
The SANADA suite is intended to extend existing speech and general-
audio benchmarks into regimes with high source density and variableK,
where many current deep learning architectures are less well tested.[9, 5]
6.1. Strengths and the Complexity Ceiling
A central strength of SANADA is its controlled generative pipeline, which
providesexplicitsourcecounts, multi-labelannotations, andperfectlyaligned
mixture–stem pairs for every clip. As shown in Section 5, attention-based
architecturessuchastheConformermaintaingoodperformanceatlowerden-
sities but exhibit a clear drop in separation, counting, and tagging quality
asKincreases.[9] For Conv-TasNet in particular, SI-SDR falls sharply be-
yondK= 4in speech mixtures, even though the model is known to perform
well on standard two- and three-speaker benchmarks. [5] This behaviour
reflects a structural property of fixed-output architectures: they assume a
known, constant number of sources at training and inference time. In con-
trast, SANADA includes both fixed-Kand variable-Ksplits, so the number
of active sources can change from clip to clip. The resulting gap points to-
wards architectures that can handle variable cardinality more directly, such
as query-based or target-conditioned separation [39, 38] and models designed
for unknownK.[40]

6.2. Limitations and Future Work
While SANADA provides a useful stress test, several limitations should
be noted:
•Monaural mixtures:Thecurrentreleaseonlycontainssingle-channel
audio. Many deployed systems rely on microphone arrays and spatial
processing for separation and denoising, so a future extension will con-
sider multi-channel versions of the same scenes.[1]
•Linguistic and acoustic coverage:The organisational speech wing
uses English lecture recordings and does not yet include conversa-
tional speech, multiple languages, or a wide range of room impulse
responses.[13] Extending SANADA in these directions would broaden
its applicability.
Within these constraints, SANADA offers a reproducible, well-documented
setting for studying separation, counting, and tagging under high acoustic
density and variable source counts.
7. Conclusion
This paper introduced SANADA, a collection of three datasets designed
to probe source separation, source counting, and multi-label tagging in high-
density acoustic scenes. By combining organisational speech (Org-Mix-4and
Org-Mix-9) with diverse environmental mixtures (FSD-Mix), the suite covers
source counts fromK= 2toK= 32within a single, unified framework. A
key component is the generative pipeline, which applies train/validation/test
partitioning at the isolated-source level before any mixing, so that evaluation
